Worldbuilding is arguably the MOST FUN distraction, it’s also necessary for every genre, even in contemporary as you build up a ‘small town’ setting for your romance, or establish a corporation or coffee shop for the setting of your tension moments. Sci-Fi requires planets, tech, aliens. Fantasy needs magic and consequences for using it. You get the idea.

Start Small, Then Expand Don’t try to build the whole world at once. Start with the location your story begins in. Expand only what your characters encounter.
Tip: Think in “camera range.” What would the character see, hear, feel in the moment?

Establish the Rules Define what’s possible in your world—magic, tech, power systems, social customs. Be consistent or establish why the rules bend or break.
Trick: Ask: “What would get someone killed, imprisoned, or exalted here?”

Ground the Extraordinary in the Familiar Use real-world analogies to make fantastical or sci-fi elements relatable. A dragon might act like a predator. A post-apocalyptic society might echo feudal power structures.
Tip: Blend one familiar element with one strange one to create depth.

Choose a Visual Aesthetic Define the look and feel of your world—architecture, fashion, colors, light, weather. Build a mood board or gather reference images for inspiration.
Trick: Pick 3 adjectives for your aesthetic (e.g., “opulent, decayed, metallic”).

Define a Sense of Time What’s the technological level? (Stone Age? Neon cyber-future?) How do people track time, celebrate, or mark history?
Tip: Anchor readers with small clues—e.g., a sunclock, a digital hive clock, an annual celebration named “Feast of the Stars.”

Culture Drives Conflict Think about how religion, politics, class, gender roles, and myths affect daily life. What’s considered normal vs taboo?
Trick: Create two clashing cultural norms that can spark character or plot tension.

Weave Worldbuilding Into the Story Don’t front-load exposition. Let readers discover the world as characters interact with it. Use sensory detail, natural dialogue, and action to reveal world elements.
Tip: Ask yourself, “How does this world affect what my character can or cannot do?”

Match Tone to World Gritty world? Use raw, sharp prose. Whimsical world? Use lyrical or quirky language. Let your tone reflect the emotional theme of your world.
Trick: Imagine your world as a soundtrack or film genre—how does it feel?
