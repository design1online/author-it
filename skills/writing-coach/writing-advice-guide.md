Curated Writing Advice Guide
Distilled from a variety of writing videos online

This guide synthesizes the key lessons from 25 writing videos. Each section lists the core advice, followed by the source video(s) that give this advice.


1. What Readers Actually Want: Storytelling Fundamentals

Zoom into the moment — don't summarize it
The single biggest difference between forgettable and compelling storytelling is specificity. Rather than saying "she was nervous before the meeting," place the reader inside the scene. Use five elements: Location (ground the reader immediately), Actions (what is happening right now), Thoughts (raw, unfiltered internal voice), Emotions (shown through the body, not just stated), and Dialogue (what people actually say). When you zoom into a single moment rather than summarizing events, readers feel like they're living it.
Source: Give me 9min, and I'll improve your storytelling skills by 176% — Philipp Humm (2.7M views)

Combat the "curse of knowledge"
Steven Pinker identifies the #1 enemy of clear writing: you already know what you're trying to say, so you forget that your reader doesn't. The fix is to write for someone who can't see inside your head. Use concrete, visual, specific language. Prose is a window, not a mirror — let the reader see what you're seeing. Read your work aloud to catch clunky phrasing. When in doubt, cut. Brevity requires less cognitive effort and almost always improves the writing.
Source: Harvard Professor Explains The Rules of Writing — Steven Pinker — David Perell (815K views)

The 4 traits that kill novels before they're finished
Bookfox identified these patterns across hundreds of manuscripts: (1) Chekhov's Gun violations — you introduce elements that never pay off; (2) Unresolved storylines — too many threads, not enough closure; (3) Kitchen sink syndrome — cramming every good idea into one book instead of saving some for later; (4) Inspiration indigestion — your book wears its influences so obviously it reads like a copy. Great novels are selective. Cut anything that doesn't serve the core story.
Source: These traits predict 90% of failed novels — Bookfox (599K views)

Lessons from pulp writers: momentum and stakes
The pulp writers of the early 20th century (Dashiell Hammett, Raymond Chandler, Max Brand) knew something modern writers forget: every scene must move the story forward. It doesn't have to escalate in size, but the reader must feel the stakes growing. Start with immediate momentum — your foot on the gas. Use foreshadowing to grip the reader instantly (promise something will happen, withhold the how and who). Sharpen every line of dialogue.
Source: 8 Storytelling Secrets Pulp Writers Knew (That We've Lost) — Bookfox (220K views)

2. Characters Readers Root For

Strong ≠ invincible
The most-watched writing video in your library has a clear thesis: a strong female character (or strong character of any kind) is not defined by competence, toughness, or never needing help. Strength comes from internal conflict — the clash between what a character wants and what they fear. A character who has everything figured out is boring. A character who is flawed, afraid, and still chooses to act is compelling. Give your protagonist something to lose internally, not just externally.
Source: How to Write a Strong Female Character — Abbie Emmons (3.4M views)

The Mary Sue problem: why perfect characters fail
The issue with a Mary Sue isn't that she's skilled — it's that she has no meaningful flaws, faces no real consequences, and the narrative rewards her without earning it. Watch for these signs: every character immediately loves or respects her, she solves every problem effortlessly, her mistakes are always someone else's fault, and she lacks a genuine internal struggle. The fix isn't to make her weaker — it's to give her wounds, blind spots, and something she genuinely can't do well.
Source: 7 Signs Your Character is a Mary Sue — Abbie Emmons (1.1M views)

Writing women (and men) authentically
Bookfox's analysis of common manuscript errors reveals that many writers — regardless of gender — reveal misunderstandings of women through specific patterns: women who exist only to validate the male protagonist, women whose motivations reduce to romance, women who talk about their appearance constantly, or women who shift personalities based on what the plot needs. The fix is to write women as people first. Give them goals unrelated to the love interest. Let them disagree, fail, and want things for their own reasons.
Source: 8 Ways Your Writing Reveals that You Don't Understand Women — Bookfox (487K views)

Make your character introductions earn their place
The difference between a good and bad character introduction often comes down to one thing: are we shown who this person is through action, or are we told through description? The moment a character does something — makes a choice, reveals a quirk, responds to pressure — readers start forming a relationship with them. Long paragraphs of physical description or backstory before the character acts are almost always a mistake.
Source: Bad Character Intros vs Good Character Intros — Writer Brandon McNulty (395K views)

3. Opening Strong: First Lines, First Pages, First Chapters

The first chapter's only job
Your first chapter doesn't need to establish world, backstory, or lore. Its only job is to make the reader need to turn the page. Abbie Emmons found that most first chapter failures come from starting too early (before the real story begins), from info-dumping about the world, and from failing to establish internal conflict. Start with your character already in motion, already wanting something, already afraid of something. Ground readers in emotion before you ground them in setting.
Source: First Chapter MISTAKES New Writers Make — Abbie Emmons (1.1M views)

11 openings that signal "amateur" to agents and readers
The most common first-chapter mistakes that cause agents to pass: (1) the dream sequence opening, (2) waking up to an alarm, (3) weather descriptions before character, (4) extended backstory or prologue before the real story, (5) characters looking in mirrors to describe themselves, (6) dialogue without context (readers don't know who's speaking or why they should care), (7) introducing too many characters at once, (8) starting in transit (character in a car/plane going somewhere), (9) the history-lesson opening (world history info-dump), (10) opening with a minor character, and (11) telegraphing the theme in the first paragraph. Each of these delays the thing readers actually came for: a person who wants something and can't easily get it.
Source: 11 Terrible Ways to Start a Novel — Bookfox (364K views)

What 100 opening lines have in common
Analyzing 100 great novel openings reveals a pattern: the best first lines create an immediate question the reader needs answered. They drop you into a specific, strange, or emotionally charged moment. They establish voice before they establish world. They promise the kind of story you're about to read. A great first line doesn't explain — it provokes.
Source: I Analysed 100 Opening Lines. Here's What Makes Readers Obsessed — Patrick Walsh (225K views)

4. Plot & Structure: The Middle Problem

Why Act 2 collapses — and how to fix it
The saggy middle happens when your protagonist stops being actively pursued by their problem and starts wandering toward a solution. The fix: your protagonist should never have more than one clear next step, and that step should always be taken away from them before they complete it. Every scene in the middle should raise a new complication or deepen the internal conflict. If you can remove a scene without affecting what comes after, the scene shouldn't exist.
Source: 9 Foolproof Strategies to Rescue Your Novel's Saggy Middle — Bookfox (240K views)

The pulp secret to reader engagement: escalating stakes
Every scene must answer the question: what does this character stand to lose right now? Not in the next chapter — right now. Momentum is built by stacking losses: small losses that compound into large ones. The reader keeps reading because they're afraid of what comes next. If you can read a chapter and feel nothing is at risk, you've found your Act 2 problem.
Source: 8 Storytelling Secrets Pulp Writers Knew — Bookfox (220K views)

5. Worldbuilding & Magic Systems

The 6 magic system mistakes that kill fantasy novels
Jed Herne's analysis of magic system failures: (1) Using magic repetitively (if your hero solves every problem the same way, it becomes boring); (2) No meaningful limitations — omnipotent characters create no tension; (3) Assuming only hard magic systems work — soft magic can be just as powerful if handled correctly; (4) Poor integration with plot — the magic should be necessary to the story, not decorative; (5) Instant mastery — a character who learns magic without struggle removes all dramatic potential; (6) Complexity without purpose — a magic system should serve the story, not show off how much you've thought about it.
Source: The 6 Worst Magic System Mistakes New Writers Make — Jed Herne (1.3M views)

9 worldbuilding mistakes every new fantasy writer makes
The core problem in new fantasy worldbuilding: writers spend enormous time building the world and very little time connecting that world to the story. The best worldbuilding is invisible — it serves the plot and character rather than existing as a backdrop you admire. Key mistakes: info-dumping history the reader doesn't need yet; creating a world so large and detailed it overwhelms rather than immerses; using anachronistic dialogue that breaks immersion; and failing to explore any aspect of the world with genuine originality. Pick two or three dimensions of your world and explore them deeply rather than touching everything shallowly.
Source: 9 Worldbuilding Mistakes Every New Writer Makes — Jed Herne (925K views)

What fantasy readers actually hate
After surveying fantasy readers, the most common complaints: protagonists who are passive rather than active, magic that conveniently solves every plot problem, world-building info dumps in the first 50 pages, characters with names so unusual they're unpronounceable, and endings that don't pay off the promises made in the opening. The bottom line: readers want characters who make interesting choices under pressure, in a world that feels lived-in rather than explained.
Source: 10 Things Fantasy Readers Hate — Jed Herne (397K views)

6. Prose, Style & Sentences

12 sentence-level mistakes that mark you as an amateur
Ellen Brock's most-referenced writing advice covers the sentence-level errors that appear in nearly every early manuscript: (1) Confusingly complex sentences — break them up; (2) Wrong tone through word choices — every word carries connotation; (3) Passive voice used by default rather than for effect; (4) Filtering (e.g., "she saw that the door was open" vs. "the door was open"); (5) Using sentence length randomly instead of for pacing; (6) Body parts taking autonomous action ("her eyes flew to the door"); (7) Was + -ing constructions that flatten urgency; (8) Unnatural word order; (9) Unnecessary similes that dilute impact; (10) Vocabulary mismatched to character or narrator; (11) Weak action verbs ("walked slowly" instead of "shuffled"); (12) Unnecessary filler words that pad without adding meaning.
Source: 12 Ways to Write Better Sentences for Creative Writers — Ellen Brock (772K views)

What separates robotic writing from professional writing
The difference between a draft that reads like a draft and one that reads like a published novel often comes down to: specificity of detail, rhythm variation, and the avoidance of throat-clearing. "Throat-clearing" is any passage where the author is warming up — explaining what's about to happen, restating what just happened, or transitioning between scenes with unnecessary summary. Cut it. Start every scene as late as possible and end it as early as possible.
Source: 8 Tricks to Take Your Writing from Robotic to Pro-Level — Bookfox (290K views)

7. Dialogue That Doesn't Kill Your Scenes

10 dialogue mistakes in fantasy fiction
Common fantasy dialogue failures: exposition disguised as conversation (characters explaining things they would already know to each other), every character sounding the same, characters being too articulate (real people speak in fragments, interruptions, and deflections), "said" avoidance (said is invisible; "exclaimed," "ejaculated," "barked" pull you out of the story), using dialect as a crutch for characterization, and dialogue without subtext (characters who always say exactly what they mean, leaving nothing beneath the surface). Great dialogue is two people talking past each other while trying to get something from the exchange.
Source: 10 Dialogue Mistakes New Fantasy Writers Make — Jed Herne (215K views)

The subtext rule
Alyssa Matesic's most actionable dialogue advice: characters should rarely say what they actually mean. Every character wants something and is afraid to ask directly. Dialogue works when that gap — between what's said and what's meant — is loaded with tension. Melodramatic dialogue (characters weeping about their feelings while explaining their backstory) fails because it removes that gap entirely. Read every dialogue exchange and ask: what are they NOT saying, and why not?
Source: 7 Dialogue Mistakes That Make Your Story Unreadable — Alyssa Matesic (140K views)

8. The Writing Process & Mindset

What 247 manuscripts have in common (the bad ones)
Patrick Walsh's most cited lesson from reviewing hundreds of manuscripts: the writers who fail all share one trait — they stop trusting their readers. This shows up as over-explaining, over-describing, spelling out the theme, and refusing to let silence or ambiguity do work. Readers are smart. When you trust them to fill in gaps, they become active participants in the story. When you explain everything, you turn them into passive recipients — and they put the book down.
Source: I Reviewed 247 Manuscripts. 7 Mistakes Ruined Most of Them — Patrick Walsh (310K views)

The most common first draft mistakes (for intermediate writers)
Ellen Brock's advice for writers past the beginner stage: the most damaging first draft mistakes are structural, not surface-level. The biggest: writing scenes that explore rather than advance (discovering character in scenes that should be cut during revision), unclear cause-and-effect chains between scenes, subplots that don't mirror or complicate the main plot, and protagonists who react rather than choose. Fix these before revising prose — there's no point polishing a structurally broken scene.
Source: 5 Common First Draft Mistakes (for intermediate to advanced writers) — Ellen Brock (195K views)

AI and writing: the real risk
The sharpest critique of AI-assisted writing isn't about the quality of the output — it's about what the process removes. Writing is a practice of noticing: noticing what you actually think, what you actually feel, what actually happened. When you outsource the finding-of-words, you outsource the noticing. The concern raised in multiple high-viewed videos: AI-generated prose is statistically average by design — it can never be strange, original, or true in the way that good writing requires.
Source: A.I. slop and the epidemic of Bad writing — Man Carrying Thing (994K views)

9. Publishing & The Industry

What agents actually look for
Alyssa Matesic's publishing advice, distilled: agents are not primarily looking for perfect prose or an original premise. They're looking for a clearly identifiable market, evidence that the author understands that market, and a story that delivers on its genre promises without being derivative. Before querying, you should be able to answer: what shelf does this go on, who exactly reads books on that shelf, and why is mine the one they'll want next? Your query letter needs to answer all three.
Source: Your Complete Guide to Publishing a Book in 2025 — Alyssa Matesic (180K views)

The query letter formula
The elements of a query letter that actually gets requests: (1) A compelling hook — one sentence that captures the premise and raises a question; (2) Character + goal + stakes — who wants what, why can't they have it, and what happens if they fail; (3) Market positioning — comp titles (two published books your novel is "the love child of"), word count, genre; (4) Brief author bio — only include credentials relevant to the book. The most common query mistakes: over-summarizing (the whole plot), under-specifying (too vague), and misidentifying the genre or audience.
Source: My Query Letter Formula That Got Book Deals — Alyssa Matesic (145K views)

10. Quick-Reference: The Principles
Below is a summary of the most universally cited principles across all your top writing videos.
* On characters: Give them internal conflict — desire vs. fear. Let them be wrong. Let them want things for their own reasons. Don't let them be the smartest person in the room unless it costs them something.
* On openings: Start as late as possible. Establish emotion before world. Every sentence of your first page should earn its place.
* On plot: Every scene raises the stakes or it shouldn't exist. Your middle is weak because your protagonist has too much breathing room.
* On prose: Read it aloud. If it sounds like writing, cut it. Specificity > generality, always.
* On dialogue: What they're not saying is more important than what they are. Cut any line where a character says exactly what they mean.
* On worldbuilding: Build what the story needs, not everything you've imagined. Your world should be inseparable from your plot.
* On the process: Trust your reader. Stop over-explaining. The gap you leave for the reader to fill is where the magic lives.
