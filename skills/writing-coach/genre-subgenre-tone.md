Choose your genre, subgenre, and tone.

Define Your Story Compass
Genre is your broad story world (sci-fi, fantasy, romance).

Subgenre narrows the flavor (cyberpunk, regency fantasy, dark romance).
Tone sets the emotional texture (gritty, whimsical, haunting, comedic).
Tip: Think of genre as the setting, subgenre as the style, and tone as the mood music.

Mix With Intention
Combine genres/subgenres wisely. (e.g., “sci-fi + horror” = Alien).

Make sure your tone supports your genre. A light tone in a tragic war story might feel jarring unless used for contrast intentionally.
Trick: Use comps (“It’s like Bridgerton meets The Hunger Games“) to test your blend.

Tone Is a Promise
Dark tone? Deliver shadows, danger, dread.

Light tone? Offer hope, humor, heart.
Be consistent—or break it with purpose for impact.
Tip: Choose 3 adjectives for your tone (e.g., “sensual, gothic, tense”) and let them guide your prose, dialogue, and pacing.

Know the Reader’s Expectations
Genre sets expectations (a romance must end in an emotionally satisfying way; a mystery must resolve the whodunit).

Subgenres often have tropes readers love. Use them… or subvert them creatively.
Trick: Study 2–3 top books in your chosen niche (tvtropes.com is a great place to start!). What tone, pacing, POV, and plot beats do they share?

Anchor Early
Make your genre/tone/subgenre clear in the first 5 pages. Readers want to know what they’ve signed up for.

Tip: Your opening scene should showcase all three (e.g., a sarcastic bounty hunter [tone] in a lawless galaxy [genre] chasing a stolen relic [subgenre: space fantasy]).

Tools to Brainstorm
Genre Wheel: Spin combinations (e.g., “noir + fantasy”).

Mood Boards: Use visuals to nail down the tone and hunt down inspiring images.
Tone Playlist: Create a writing soundtrack that matches the emotional feel you’re aiming for.

Stay Flexible—Then Commit
If you’re stuck, try writing your opening in two different tones or subgenres to see what flows best.

But once chosen, commit—consistency builds immersion.

Work Backward From the Ending
Know your genre/subgenre so your ending feels earned. For instance, if you’re writing a survival horror, the ending tone should match the expectations or subvert well-worn cliches.

Tip: Ask: What emotion do I want readers to feel when they close the book?
