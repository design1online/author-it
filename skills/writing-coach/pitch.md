Here are different formats for pitching a book. Each of these should be no more than 2-3 sentences.

High concept pitch:
Format: A [flawed protagonist] must [impossible task] or [specific cost].

Elevator pitch:
Format: When [situation], [character] must [goal] in order to [stakes].

Character pitch:
Format: A [flawed protagonist] who wants [goal] because [motivation], but fears [deepest fear], shaped by [crucial backstory piece].

Premise pitch:
Format: [flawed protagonist] experiences [inciting incident] they must [impossible choice + stakes].
A strong pitch typically includes:

Protagonist (who)
Goal (what they want)
Obstacle (what stands in their way)
Stakes (why it matters)

No conflict = no story.

Ask: Who wants something, what’s stopping them, and what will they lose if they fail?
Fill in this template: When [inciting event], a [flawed protagonist] must [goal] before [stakes].
“When her kingdom is invaded, an exiled princess must lead a brutal army or lose her people forever.”
Cut the Fluff

Avoid names and backstory in your logline.

Use archetypes or titles instead of names: “a cursed mercenary,” “a rebellious noble,” “a haunted detective.”
Trick: Try shrinking your sentence to 20 words or less—tightness forces clarity.

Think Cinematic
Imagine your story as a movie trailer. What’s the hook? What’s the high-stakes moment?
Ask: Would this sentence make someone want to watch the story unfold?
